Executive Summary 
The second half of the year presents a critical opportunity for the I.T Department to consolidate 
achievements, optimize operational costs, and position the organization for sustainable growth. 
The Group Chief Technology Officer (CTO) will focus on improving infrastructure resilience, 
enhancing cybersecurity, increasing operational efficiency, and delivering measurable business 
value through technology. 
The strategic direction for the remaining six months is centered around nine major pillars: 
1. Network Infrastructure Upgrade 
2. Cybersecurity Enhancement  
3. IT Cost Optimization  
4. Virtualization & Cloud Optimization  
5. Data Centre Actualization  
6. Human Capital Development  
7. Software Development & Automation  
8. Power Backup 
9. I.T Governance, Risk & Compliance

Expected Outcomes by Year-End 
By executing this strategic plan over the final six months of the year, the CTO's office aims to 
achieve: 
• 
Technology Excellence: 99.95% network availability, improved system resilience, and 
reduced service interruptions.  
• 
Cybersecurity Maturity: Stronger defenses through Zero Trust principles, enhanced 
monitoring, and proactive threat management.  
• 
Operational Efficiency: Reduced manual processes, faster service delivery, and 
improved IT support performance through automation.  
• 
Financial Optimization: Lower operating costs through bandwidth optimization, 
virtualization, storage management, and energy efficiency.  
• 
Infrastructure Modernization: Upgraded network, virtualized infrastructure, and a 
more resilient, scalable data center.  
• 
Workforce Capability: A highly skilled technology team equipped with certifications 
and competencies in network, cloud, cybersecurity, automation, and leadership.  
• 
Governance and Compliance: Improved IT governance, risk management, business 
continuity, and audit readiness.  
• 
Business Value: Technology positioned as a strategic enabler that supports 
organizational growth, innovation, and long-term sustainability.  
Final Note: 
This report provides a structured executive roadmap for the CTO to drive measurable 
performance improvements during the last six months of the year while aligning technology 
initiatives with organizational objectives and delivering clear value to executive management of 
the EIB GROUP of companies.

RFI stands for Request for Information. 


Page 10:
In construction projects, an RFI is a formal document used by the site team, 
contractor, or supervisor to seek clarification when drawings, specifications, or 
instructions are unclear, incomplete, or conflicting. 
Common Situations That Require an RFI 
• Architectural drawing conflicts with structural drawing. 
• Dimensions are missing from drawings. 
• Material specifications are unclear. 
• Site conditions differ from the design. 
• The contractor needs clarification before proceeding with work. 
Example 
A site supervisor notices that: 
• The architectural drawing shows a wall thickness of 225mm. 
• The structural drawing shows 150mm. 
The supervisor submits an RFI to the Design Unit asking: 
"Please confirm the correct wall thickness for Grid Line B-C between Axes 3 
and 5, as the architectural and structural drawings show different dimensions." 
The Design Unit reviews the issue and responds with the correct specification.

Benefits of Using RFIs 
• Prevents costly mistakes and rework. 
• Creates a documented record of decisions. 
• Improves communication between site and design teams. 
• Protects the company during disputes or claims. 
• Ensures construction follows approved designs.

Best practice in effective building construction project delivery is not a single 
technique; it is a combination of planning, management, communication, 
quality control, and risk management throughout the project lifecycle. 
1. Define Clear Project Objectives 
Before construction begins, establish: 
• Scope of work 
• Budget and funding arrangements 
• Schedule and milestones 
• Quality standards 
• Safety requirements 
• Stakeholder expectations 
A well-defined project brief reduces misunderstandings and costly changes later. 
2. Conduct Thorough Planning and Design 
Effective projects invest significant effort in pre-construction planning: 
• Site investigations and surveys 
• Feasibility studies 
• Detailed architectural and engineering designs 
• Constructability reviews 
• Regulatory approvals and permits 
Resolving design issues before construction starts minimizes delays and rework. 
3. Use Competent Project Teams 
Select qualified: 
• Architects 
• Engineers 
• Quantity surveyors 
• Contractors 
• Project managers 


Page 13:
• Specialized subcontractors 
Competence and experience often have a greater impact on project success than 
low bid prices. 
4. Establish Strong Project Governance 
Create clear: 
• Roles and responsibilities 
• Reporting structures 
• Decision-making processes 
• Approval procedures 
A governance framework helps maintain accountability and prevents confusion. 
5. Implement Effective Communication 
Maintain regular communication among: 
• Client 
• Consultants 
• Contractors 
• Suppliers 
• Regulatory authorities 
Best practices include: 
• Weekly progress meetings 
• Written reports 
• Document control systems 
• Digital collaboration platforms 
6. Develop Realistic Schedules 
Use scheduling tools such as: 
• Critical Path Method (CPM) 
• Gantt charts 


Page 14:
• Look-ahead planning 
Monitor progress continuously and update schedules when conditions change. 
7. Practice Cost Management 
Control costs through: 
• Accurate estimating 
• Budget monitoring 
• Value engineering 
• Change order management 
• Cash flow planning 
Regular cost reviews help prevent budget overruns. 
8. Prioritize Quality Management 
Establish a Quality Management Plan that includes: 
• Material inspections 
• Workmanship standards 
• Testing and commissioning 
• Quality audits 
• Defect management procedures 
Quality should be built into the process rather than inspected at the end. 
9. Manage Risks Proactively 
Identify and monitor risks related to: 
• Weather 
• Labor shortages 
• Material price fluctuations 
• Design changes 
• Safety incidents 
• Supply chain disruptions 


Page 15:
Maintain a risk register and assign mitigation measures. 
10. Maintain Strong Health, Safety, and Environmental (HSE) Standards 
Key practices include: 
• Site safety plans 
• Worker training 
• Hazard assessments 
• Emergency response procedures 
• Environmental protection measures 
Good safety performance often correlates with better overall project 
performance. 
11. Utilize Technology 
Modern construction projects benefit from: 
• Building Information Modeling (BIM) 
• Drones for site monitoring 
• Digital project management systems 
• Mobile field reporting tools 
• Cloud-based document management 
Technology improves coordination, accuracy, and decision-making. 
12. Monitor and Control Project Performance 
Track: 
• Schedule performance 
• Cost performance 
• Quality metrics 
• Safety indicators 
• Productivity rates 
Early identification of issues allows corrective action before problems escalate. 


Page 16:
13. Manage Changes Carefully 
Construction changes are often unavoidable, but they should: 
• Be documented formally 
• Include cost and schedule impacts 
• Receive proper approvals 
• Be communicated to all affected parties 
Uncontrolled changes are a major cause of project failure. 
14. Ensure Proper Project Closeout 
Before handover: 
• Complete inspections 
• Resolve defects and punch-list items 
• Obtain regulatory approvals 
• Deliver as-built drawings 
• Provide operation and maintenance manuals 
• Train facility operators 
Summary 
The most effective building construction project delivery approach can be 
summarized as: 
Plan thoroughly → Select competent teams → Communicate effectively → 
Control cost, time, quality, and safety → Manage risks proactively → Use 
technology → Execute disciplined project closeout. 
Projects that focus on these principles consistently achieve better outcomes in 
terms of schedule, budget, quality, safety, and client satisfaction.

WHAT IS STERILE ROOM 
A sterile room in a hospital is a highly controlled environment designed to 
eliminate or minimize all forms of microorganisms, including bacteria, viruses, 
and fungi, to prevent contamination during medical procedures and the 
processing of medical devices. These rooms, such as operating rooms and 
sterile processing departments, use advanced air filtration, strict cleaning 
protocols, and controlled temperature and humidity to maintain an aseptic 
environment for critical medical tasks.

· Preventing Infection: 
The primary purpose is to create a contamination-free zone to protect patients, 
especially those undergoing surgery or with compromised immune systems.  
  
· Sterilizing Equipment:

decontaminating, assembling, and packaging surgical instruments for reuse.  
  
· Aseptic Procedures:

TRAUMA ROOM  
A trauma room is a specially designed part of a hospital's emergency 
department (ER) focused on treating severe, life-threatening injuries. Also 
known as an Emergency Trauma Room (ETR), it is equipped with advanced 
resources and staff, including trauma surgeons and critical care specialists, to 
provide immediate, life-saving care for patients suffering from events like 
severe car accidents or gunshot wounds. These rooms are distinct from a 
standard ER, as they are part of a specialized trauma center designed to handle 
the most critical injuries.  
 
 
 
TRAUMA ROOM 
KEY CHARACTERISTICS OF A TRAUMA ROOM 
· 
Focus: Specializes in treating severe, traumatic injuries.  
  
· 
Staff: Has highly trained staff, including trauma surgeons, critical care 
specialists, and emergency medical staff.  
  
· 
Equipment: Equipped with advanced medical technology to provide 
rapid diagnosis and intervention.

·

TRAUMA ROOMS OR STANDARD EMERGENCY ROOMS (ERS)  
  
· 
Scope of Care: 
A standard ER treats a wide range of medical issues, from minor to severe, 
while a trauma room is dedicated to the most critical, life-threatening cases. 
· 
Level of Specialization: 
Trauma centers have specialized facilities, staff, and protocols for handling 
severe trauma, which not all hospitals with an ER possess. 
· 
Designation: 
Hospitals with trauma rooms are often designated as trauma centers, which are 
categorized by levels (I to IV) based on their resources and capabilities to 
handle severe injuries.

A hospital imaging room, also known as a radiology room, is a specialized 
space within a hospital designed for diagnostic imaging procedures like X-rays,

ensure patient safety and staff protection from radiation, such as thick walls, 
lead shielding, and non-porous floors. The design also considers workflow 
efficiency, with proper spacing, lighting, ventilation, and accessibility for 
patients and equipment.

An operating theatre procedure in the hospital refers to the series of steps

• Patient assessment 
o Confirm identity, allergies, medical history, consent. 
o Conduct necessary lab tests (blood, imaging, etc.). 
• Pre-op checklist 
o Fasting (NPO status). 
o Site marking (for correct surgery site). 
o Removal of jewelry, dentures, nail polish, etc.

• Theatre preparation 
o Sterilization of instruments. 
o Ensuring availability of required equipment and implants. 
 2. Intra-Operative Phase (Inside Operating Theatre) 
• Patient transfer & positioning 
o Secure and position the patient safely on the operating table. 
o Attach monitoring devices (ECG, BP cuff, pulse oximeter). 
• Anesthesia 
o General, regional, or local anesthesia administered by anesthetist. 
• Aseptic technique 
o Surgical team scrubs, gowns, and gloves. 
o Sterile draping of the patient. 
• Surgical procedure 
o Incision, operation, and necessary interventions. 
o Hemostasis (controlling bleeding). 
• Instrument & swab count (to avoid retained foreign objects). 
• Closure 
o Suturing or stapling. 
o Dressing application. 
 3. Post-Operative Phase 
• Immediate care 
o Patient transferred to recovery room (PACU). 
o Monitoring vital signs, airway, pain management.

situated next to the operating room (OR) where surgical teams perform a

touchless sinks, antimicrobial surfaces, and integrated soap and disinfectant 
dispensers, it serves as a critical barrier to prevent infection, ensuring staff are 
both physically hygienic and mentally prepared for surgery.

FUNCTION AND PURPOSE  
· 
Infection Prevention: 
The primary role of a scrub room is to uphold aseptic technique by initiating the 
sterile process, thereby reducing the risk of surgical site infections (SSIs) and 
cross-contamination. 
 
· 
Transitional Zone: 
It acts as the definitive point of separation between the non-sterile areas of the 
hospital and the highly sterile environment of the OR. 
 
· 
PPE Checkpoint: 
The room serves as the last checkpoint for personal protective equipment (PPE) 
and hygiene practices, ensuring all surgical staff are properly prepared. 
 
· 
Mental Readiness: 
Beyond physical hygiene, the room provides psychological readiness for 
surgical teams, bridging patient safety with workflow. 
KEY DESIGN FEATURES 
· 
Specialized Sinks: 
Scrub rooms are equipped with surgical scrub sinks, also known as scrub 
stations, which are larger, more robust, and made of high-grade stainless steel or 
antibacterial materials.  
 
· 
Touchless Operation: 
Fittings on these sinks are touchless, operated by sensors or knee levers to 
prevent the transfer of germs from hands to surfaces.  
  
· 
Disinfectant and Soap Dispensers: 
Touchless dispensers for antiseptic soaps and disinfectants are built into the 
scrub stations.  
  
· 
Low-Splash Water: 
The water flow is designed to be even and low-splash, preventing the spread of 
microorganisms.

X-RAY MACHINE  
 
What is an X-ray? 
An X-ray is a type of medical imaging that uses radiation to take pictures of the inside 
of your body. We often think of X-rays as something providers use to diagnose broken 
bones or look at your teeth. But X-ray images can also help providers diagnose a wide 
range of injuries, disorders and diseases. 
Most people use the term “X-ray” to mean a plain radiograph (plain film), or just one 
image. But X-rays are also the type of radiation that imaging machines use to create 
pictures. X-ray radiation is also used in other types of medical imaging, like CT scans, 
to get many images that are interpreted by a computer to form 3D pictures. This piece 
will focus on plain X-rays. 
 
What is an X-ray with contrast material? 
Some X-rays use contrast material (also called contrast agent or dye). It makes certain 
structures in your body, like blood vessels, easier to see. 
The contrast material comes as a liquid, powder or pill. Your provider gives you the 
contrast material before the X-ray. Depending on the type of X-ray, you may receive 
the contrast material: 


Page 26:
 
 
Research Unit 
· 
Orally (by mouth). 
· 
Through an injection into a vein (IV) or your cerebrospinal fluid. 
· 
By inserting it into your rectum (enema). 
 
When do you need an X-ray? 
Your provider may order an X-ray to: 
· 
Check for a broken bone (fracture). 
· 
Identify the cause of symptoms, such as pain and swelling. 
· 
Look for signs of infection in your lungs. 
· 
Look for foreign objects in your body. 
· 
Look for structural problems in your bones, joints or soft tissues. 
· 
Plan and evaluate treatments. 
 
What can an X-ray show? 
Issues that can show up on X-rays include: 
· 
Arthritis. 
· 
Broken bones. 
· 
Bone changes or abnormalities. 
· 
Herniated discs in your spine. 
· 
Infections. 
· 
Kidney stones. 
· 
Scoliosis and other spine curvature conditions. 
· 
Tooth cavities. 
· 
Tumors. 
 
CAN AN X-RAY SHOW CANCER? 
X-rays can show cancer, but it’s not the primary way that providers look for or 
diagnose most cancers. This is because tumors in your organs can be small or hidden 
behind other structures in your body (like your ribs in the case of a chest X-ray) or 
blend in with normal tissues. 
 
WHAT ARE THE TYPES OF X-RAYS? 
Several types of X-rays take pictures of different areas inside your body. Some of the 
most common types of X-rays include: 
· 
Abdominal X-ray. This X-ray helps providers evaluate parts of your digestive 
system and diagnose conditions like kidney stones and bladder stones. 
· 
Bone X-ray. You might get a bone X-ray if your provider suspects you have a 
broken bone, dislocated joint or arthritis. Images from bone X-rays can also show 
signs of bone cancer or infection. 


Page 27:
 
 
Research Unit 
· 
Chest X-ray. Your provider might order a chest X-ray if you have symptoms 
like chest pain, shortness of breath or a cough. 
· 
Dental X-ray. A dental provider takes regular X-rays of your mouth to look for 
issues with your teeth and gums. 
· 
Head X-ray. These can help providers see skull fractures from head injuries or 
conditions that affect how the bones in your skull form, like craniosynostosis. 
· 
Spine X-ray. A provider can use a spine X-ray to look for spine curvatures, 
slipped discs and other spine issues. 
 
Other types of medical imaging use a series of X-rays interpreted by a computer 
to make detailed images. These include: 
· 
Bone density (DXA) scan. 
· 
CT scan (computed tomography). 
· 
Fluoroscopy. 
· 
Mammogram. 
 
HOW DO X-RAYS WORK? 
X-rays work by sending beams of radiation through your body to create images on an 
X-ray detector nearby. Radiation beams are invisible, and you can’t feel them. 
As the beams go through your body, bones, soft tissues and other structures absorb 
radiation in different ways. Solid or dense objects (such as bones) absorb radiation 
easily, so they appear bright white on the image. Soft tissues (such as organs) don’t 
absorb radiation as easily, so they appear in shades of gray on the X-ray. 
A radiologist interprets the image and writes a report for the physician who ordered 
the X-ray, noting if there’s anything in the image that’s abnormal or concerning. 
 
HOW DO I PREPARE FOR AN X-RAY? 
Preparation for an X-ray depends on the type of X-ray you’re getting. Your provider 
may ask you to: 
· 
Avoid using lotions, creams or perfume. These can cause shadows on your X-
rays and make the results inaccurate. 
· 
Remove metal objects like jewelry, hairpins or hearing aids. Metal can interfere 
with X-rays and make the results inaccurate. 
· 
Stop eating or drinking several hours beforehand. 
· 
Wear comfortable clothing or change into a gown before the X-ray. 
 
WHAT CAN I EXPECT DURING AN X-RAY? 
The exact steps of an X-ray depend on what kind of X-ray you’re getting. In general, 
during an X-ray your provider will:

1. 
Give you contrast medium (if necessary) through an injection or enema, or 
have you swallow it. You may feel flushed or warm for a little while after an IV 
injection of dye. Some people get a metallic taste in their mouth. These side effects go 
away in a few minutes. 
2. 
Ask you to sit, stand or lie down on a table. In the past, your provider may have 
covered you with a heavy lead shield (apron), but new evidence suggests that they 
aren’t necessary. 
3. 
Position the camera near the body part that they’re getting a picture of. 
4.

WHAT HAPPENS AFTER AN X-RAY? 
Most of the time, there aren’t any restrictions on what you can do after an X-ray. If 
you received contrast medium, your provider might give you special instructions, like 
drinking plenty of water afterwards. 
 
WHAT ARE THE RISKS OR SIDE EFFECTS OF X-RAYS? 
X-rays are generally safe and low-risk. Some people have side effects from contrast 
dye, which may include: 
· 
Nausea or vomiting. 
· 
Stomach cramps or diarrhea. 
· 
Headaches. 
There’s a small risk of an allergic reaction to the contrast material. People who have 
allergies or asthma are more likely to have an allergic reaction to contrast dye. Talk to 
your provider about your risk of a reaction, and call them right away if you have 
unusual symptoms after an X-ray with contrast. 
Although X-rays use radiation (which can cause cancer and other health problems), 
you’re exposed to a very small amount during a plain radiograph — usually not much 
more than you’d get just from naturally occurring radiation you’re exposed to in your 
daily life (background radiation).  
For instance, a chest X-ray exposes you to about 10 days’ worth of background 
radiation. A dental X-ray exposes you to about one day of background radiation. 
 
X-RAYS IN PREGNANCY 
While the amount of radiation exposure to an adult during an X-ray isn’t usually 
harmful, it can harm a fetus. If you’re pregnant, your provider may choose another 
imaging study, such as MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) or ultrasound. 
 
WHAT TYPE OF RESULTS DO YOU GET FROM AN X-RAY? 
A radiologist will review your X-ray and note any findings. They may note if they 
recommend a follow-up. Your healthcare provider will receive both the images and

HOW LONG DOES IT TAKE TO GET X-RAY RESULTS? 
Results from a bone X-ray are often ready right away, but the time it takes for a 
radiologist to look at them can vary. Your provider might share your results with you 
after the X-ray, or you might see them show up in your electronic health records. 
Results from other types of X-rays (such as a GI test) may take longer. Talk to your 
provider about when you can expect results. 
 
IF THE RESULTS ARE ABNORMAL WHAT ARE THE NEXT STEPS? 
Your provider will let you know if you need follow-up imaging or testing. Not all 
abnormal findings are concerning — in fact, many incidental findings are benign (not 
harmful). But check with your provider if you have any questions about the X-ray 
results.

· 
Headaches.

Nausea or vomiting. 
· 
Trouble breathing or shortness of breath.

 The capacity of a water treatment plant is an important indicator for evaluating its

plant is crucial for practitioners in the water treatment industry and engineers in 
related fields. 
This article will explore in detail the calculation method of water treatment plant 
capacity and analyze its size and practical application using a 3000 liter/hour water 
treatment plant as an example.

What Is The Capacity Of A Water Treatment Plant? 
The capacity of a water treatment plant usually refers to the amount of water that the 
water treatment equipment can treat per unit time, usually expressed in liters/hour 
(L/h), cubic meters/hour (m³/h) or million gallons/day (MGD). This indicator reflects 
the maximum treatment capacity of a water treatment plant under its design conditions 
and is an important basis for the design and operation of water treatment facilities. 
The Capacity of a Water Treatment Plant Is Affected By Several Factors, 
Including: 
● Water quality: The higher the degree of contamination of the raw water, the 
greater the complexity of the treatment process, which affects the treatment capacity. 
● Treatment process: Different water treatment technologies (such as reverse 
osmosis, ultrafiltration, sedimentation, disinfection, etc.) have different requirements 
for water treatment speed and efficiency. 


Page 31:
 
 
Research Unit 
● Equipment efficiency: The performance, maintenance status and operating 
conditions of the equipment will affect the actual treatment capacity. 
● Operating parameters: Operating conditions such as flow, pressure, temperature, 
etc. will also affect the capacity of the water treatment plant. 
 
How To Calculate the Capacity of a Water Treatment Plant? 
To calculate the capacity of a water treatment plant, it is necessary to comprehensively 
consider the design parameters, process flow and actual operating conditions. The 
following are the main steps to calculate the capacity: 
Determine The Design Flow 
The design flow is the basis for calculating the capacity of a water treatment plant. 
The design flow is usually determined based on demand analysis, including factors 
such as water consumption, water supply range and water quality requirements. 
Taking 3000 liters/hour as an example, the design flow is 3000 liters/hour, or 3 cubic 
meters/hour. 
Determine The Treatment Process and Flow 
The treatment process of a water treatment plant usually includes multiple steps, such 
as pretreatment (screening, sedimentation), primary treatment (reverse osmosis, 
ultrafiltration), post-treatment (disinfection, mineralization), etc. The treatment 


Page 32:
 
 
Research Unit 
capacity of different process steps may be different, so the treatment capacity of each 
step needs to be considered comprehensively when calculating the capacity. 
 
For example, if a water treatment plant uses multi-stage filtration, reverse osmosis and 
disinfection processes, the treatment capacity of each step may be as follows: 
● Multi-stage filtration: 5 cubic meters/hour 
● Reverse osmosis: 3 cubic meters/hour 
● Disinfection: 4 cubic meters/hour 
Since reverse osmosis is the bottleneck process and has the lowest treatment capacity, 
the actual capacity of the water treatment plant should be calculated as 3 cubic 
meters/hour. 
 
CONSIDER WATER LOSS AND BY-PRODUCTS 
In actual operation, the water treatment process usually produces a certain amount of 
water loss or by-products.  
For example, the reverse osmosis system usually produces a certain proportion of 
concentrated water, which cannot be used as purified water. Therefore, this part of the 
loss needs to be considered when calculating the capacity. 
Taking a 3000 liter/hour water treatment plant as an example, assuming that the water 
production rate of the reverse osmosis system is 75%, that is, for every 100 liters of 
raw water processed, 75 liters of purified water are finally produced. Then, the actual 
water purification capacity of the system is: 3000L/h×75%=2250L/h. 
That is, 2250 liters of purified water can be produced per hour.

In actual operation, equipment utilization and redundant design are also important 
factors affecting capacity. Equipment utilization refers to the actual operating capacity 
of the equipment under design conditions, which is usually less than 100%. 
Redundant design is the backup capacity set to cope with equipment maintenance, 
failure or peak demand.

What Is Building Settlement?
Building settlement refers to the downward movement of a structure's foundation due to compression, 
consolidation, or displacement of the underlying soil or rock. It is a natural geotechnical phenomenon that must be 
monitored and controlled to ensure structural safety.
Normal Range
25 – 50 mm
Typical acceptable settlement for most 
structures on cohesive soils
Differential Risk
1 : 300
Angular distortion ratio that triggers 
visible cracking in buildings
Monitoring Period
Years
Settlement may continue for decades in 
soft clay soils


Page 80:
Types of Settlement
01  Immediate Settlement
•
Occurs instantly upon load application
•
Primarily elastic deformation of soil
•
More pronounced in sandy & granular soils
•
Also called elastic or elastic-plastic settlement
02  Primary Consolidation
•
Gradual expulsion of water from saturated clay
•
Can continue for years or even decades
•
Governed by Terzaghi's consolidation theory
•
Dominant in soft, compressible clay layers
03  Secondary Compression
•
Occurs after primary consolidation is complete
•
Due to plastic rearrangement of soil particles
•
Significant in organic soils and peats
•
Also called creep settlement
04  Differential Settlement
•
Unequal settlement across the foundation
•
Most damaging type — causes structural distress
•
Results from variable soil or loading conditions
•
Leads to tilting, cracking & structural failure

U-Value (Thermal Transmittance)
Measures heat flow through glass. The lower the U-value, the better the insulation. Building regulations 
typically require U ≤ 1.4–1.6 W/m²K for windows.
Solar Heat Gain Coefficient (g)
Proportion of solar energy passing through glass. Low-g glass (0.2–0.4) suits hot climates; high-g glass (0.5–0.7) 
is beneficial for passive solar heating in cold climates.
Low-E Coatings
Microscopically thin metallic layer (silver or titanium oxide) applied by magnetron sputtering. Reflects long-
wave infrared radiation back into the room, dramatically reducing heat loss.
Thermal Bridging at Frames
Warm-edge spacer bars in IGUs and thermally broken window frames prevent condensation and reduce heat 
loss at junctions between glass and structure.

04  |  BASE PLATES
What is a Base Plate?
A base plate is a thick flat steel plate placed between the base of a structural steel column 
and the concrete foundation or footing. It distributes the column load over a larger area, 
ensuring the bearing stress on the foundation material remains within safe limits. Base 
plates are welded or bolted to the column and anchored to the foundation via anchor bolts.

Steel Reinforcement Research Summary
1
Steel reinforcement comes in many forms — from deformed rebar and WWM to full 
structural sections — each suited to specific load conditions.
2
I-sections (UB) excel in bending-dominated beams; H-sections (UC) are optimised for 
columns under combined axial and bending loads.
3
C-sections, T-sections, and angle bars serve as secondary structural members, purlins, 
bracing, and connection elements.
4
Hollow sections (RHS/SHS/CHS) provide exceptional torsional stiffness and 
aesthetic appeal for modern architecture.
5
Base plates are critical load transfer elements: their thickness and size must be engineered 
to distribute column forces safely to foundations.
6
Always select steel grade (S275/S355/Fe500) and section size based on structural 
calculations per applicable codes (BS EN, IS, ASTM).
For structural design always consult a licensed structural engineer and refer to applicable national design codes.

LAYER BY LAYER
① CONDUCTOR
Carries the electrical current. Made of copper (Cu) or aluminium (Al). Can be solid or stranded.
Stranded conductors are more flexible. Cross-section measured in mm² or AWG.
② CONDUCTOR SCREEN
Semiconducting layer on HV cables. Smooths the electric field around the conductor to prevent
ionisation and hot spots. Critical above 3.3kV.
③ INSULATION
Electrically isolates the conductor. Materials: PVC (low voltage), XLPE (medium/high voltage), EPR,
paper. Thickness determines voltage rating.
④ INSULATION SCREEN
Second semiconducting layer (HV cables). Ensures uniform electric field distribution. Bonded or
unbonded to insulation.
⑤ METAL SCREEN / SHIELD
Copper tape, wires, or foil. Provides earthing path, reduces electromagnetic interference (EMI), and
carries fault currents.
⑥ BEDDING / FILLERS
Binds cores together, fills voids, gives the cable a round profile. Made of PVC, rubber, or polypropylene
filler rope.
